Spatial Latent Field Terrorism

This repository presents a spatial–temporal analysis of terrorist activity using Bayesian hierarchical modelling. The project applies Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (INLA) and Stochastic Partial Differential Equation (SPDE) latent field models to uncover hidden spatial opportunity structures that shape where and how terrorist attacks occur. Moving beyond observable covariates, the analysis focuses on identifying latent spatial processes that govern patterns of violence across conflict environments.

The central research question guiding this work is whether terrorist groups operate within their own hidden spatial opportunity structures. To address this, the modelling framework decomposes spatial variation into shared and group-specific components. Shared latent fields capture common environmental and structural features of conflict zones, while group-specific latent fields allow for the identification of distinct operational geographies. This hierarchical approach enables a deeper understanding of how different groups interact with space and whether their behaviour is constrained by common or unique opportunity landscapes.

The project contributes to theoretical developments in constraint-driven terrorism dynamics and spatial opportunity structure theory. A key insight emerging from the analysis is that terrorist activity is primarily shaped by latent spatial environments rather than solely by ideology or observable characteristics. This supports a broader shift toward viewing terrorism as an adaptive process embedded within spatially structured opportunity systems.

Methodologically, the project employs Bayesian inference using INLA, combined with SPDE-based latent Gaussian random fields to model spatial dependence. Model comparison is conducted using metrics such as the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), Watanabe-Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC), and Conditional Predictive Ordinates (CPO). The modelling strategy progresses from baseline covariate-only models, to models incorporating a shared spatial field, and finally to models that include both shared and group-specific latent fields. This layered approach allows for systematic evaluation of how spatial structure contributes to model performance and explanatory power.

The analysis incorporates a range of spatial and environmental features, including projected coordinate systems (UTM), distances to national and provincial borders, distances to capital cities, and terrain characteristics such as elevation and slope. These variables provide a foundation for modelling observable influences, while the latent fields capture unobserved spatial processes that cannot be directly measured.

The repository is structured to support reproducibility and extensibility, with directories for data, scripts, custom functions, outputs, and visualisations. Users can replicate the analysis by installing the required R packages, constructing spatial projections, building the SPDE mesh, and fitting the sequence of models. Outputs include posterior summaries of latent spatial fields, model fit statistics, and predicted risk surfaces that highlight areas of elevated terrorist activity.

The findings have important applications for understanding the spatial diffusion of violence, identifying hotspots, and informing counter-terrorism strategies. By distinguishing between shared and group-specific spatial dynamics, the project provides a framework for analysing how different organisations exploit or adapt to their environments. Future work will extend this approach by incorporating spatio-temporal dynamics, counter-terrorism exposure variables, and cross-country comparative analysis.
